Chapter 7: Reasoning Prompts

"Before giving me your recommendation on [X], walk me through your reasoning step by step. Identify what you're assuming, what evidence you're weighing, and where your confidence is lower. Then give me your conclusion."

"Now take the position of a rigorous critic of the recommendation above. What are the strongest objections to this analysis? What would someone need to believe to find this recommendation unconvincing?"

"I am about to recommend [X] to [audience] for [reason]. Your job is to argue against this recommendation as forcefully as possible. Do not hedge. Make the strongest case against it using whatever valid arguments apply. Do not conclude by restoring confidence in my position. Give me the full counterargument."

"Review the following plan, recommendation, or analysis. List every assumption it makes. Explicit and implicit. For each assumption, rate its confidence level as high, medium, or low, and note whether it is tested or untested in the current analysis."

"Assume it is six months from now and [plan, recommendation, or project] has failed significantly. You don't know why it failed yet. What are the three to five most plausible failure modes? For each, describe the specific mechanism by which that failure would occur."

Chapter 10: The Communication Playbook

Workflow 1: Stakeholder Email

Step 1: Build the recipient profile. "I need to write an email to [role/name]. Here is what they already know about this situation: [context]. Here is what they care most about in their role: [priorities]. Here is the decision or response I need from them: [objective]. Before drafting anything, give me a three-sentence summary of what this person needs to hear to take that action."

Step 2: Supply Voice Anchors and request a calibrated draft. "Now draft the email. Use these voice patterns as anchors for my writing style: [paste two or three characteristic phrases or sentence patterns from real emails sent]. The email should be [tone register]. Do not use filler openings. Do not hedge on the ask. Length: [specify]."

Step 3: Run a recipient reaction check. "Read this email as [recipient role]. What is their first reaction? What question does this email leave unanswered? What, if anything, would make them hesitant to respond as requested?"

Step 4: Request targeted revisions. "Revise paragraph [N] to [specific instruction]. Do not change the rest of the email."

Workflow 2: Executive Brief

Step 1: Situation distillation. "Here is the raw material for a situation I need to brief a senior leader on: [paste material]. In three sentences, summarize: what is happening, what decision is needed, and what happens if no decision is made. Do not add framing or recommendations yet."

Step 2: Executive context calibration. "The audience for this brief is [role]. Their known priorities are [list]. They typically prefer [communication style: data-first, narrative-first, or options-first]. The decision I need them to make is [specific decision]. Given this, which of the following brief structures will land best: (a) Situation, Recommendation, Evidence; (b) Problem, Options, Recommended Path; or (c) Context, Decision Required, Implications of Each Choice? Explain your reasoning in one paragraph."

Step 3: Draft using the selected structure. "Draft a one-page brief using structure [selected]. Use plain language. No jargon. Limit to [X] words. Lead with the decision needed rather than the background. Include one section for supporting evidence and one for risks or open questions."

Step 4: Apply the 'So what' test. "Read this brief as the executive receiving it. After reading each section, state what question a sharp reader would ask. Identify any section where the answer to 'so what?' is not explicit in the text."

Step 5: Compress and finalize. "This brief is currently [X] words. Cut it to [target] words without removing any decision-relevant information. Flag any cut that removes something you believe is decision-relevant so I can review it."

Workflow 3: Difficult Conversation Preparation

Step 1: Neutral situation summary. "Here is a difficult conversation I need to prepare for: [situation description]. Summarize the situation in three sentences from a neutral third-party perspective. No advocacy for either side. Then identify the two or three core tensions this conversation needs to address."

Step 2: Map the other person's perspective. "Describe how [other person's role] is likely experiencing this situation. What do they believe is true? What are they protecting? What outcome do they most want from this conversation? What would make them defensive?"

Step 3: Draft opening language. "Draft three different ways I could open this conversation. Each should: name the issue without accusation, signal that I want to understand their perspective, and sound like me rather than a script. Here are my voice patterns: [Voice Anchors]."

Step 4: Generate response paths. "If the conversation goes in one of three directions (they agree with my framing, they push back, or they disengage), what is the appropriate response to each? Give me one or two sentences for each path rather than a full script."

Step 5: Identify agreements needed. "What specific agreements or acknowledgments do I need from this conversation to move forward? What am I willing to adjust or concede, and what is non-negotiable?"

Workflow 4: Presentation Architecture

Step 1: Audience and objective brief. "I am building a presentation for [audience description]. Their current position is [what they believe now]. After this presentation, I need them to [objective]. The main objection they are likely to raise is [anticipated resistance]. Confirm you understand these parameters before I continue."

Step 2: Narrative arc selection. "Given this audience and objective, which of the following narrative structures will be most effective: (a) Problem, Consequence, Solution, Call to Action; (b) Current State, Desired State, Gap, Path; (c) Insight, Implication, Recommendation, Evidence; or (d) Story, Stakes, Options, Decision? Explain why the other structures would be less effective for this specific audience."

Step 3: Build the argument spine. "Using structure [selected], draft the argument spine for this presentation. For each section: a single governing claim in one sentence, the evidence or reasoning that supports it, and the transition logic to the next section. Do not write slide content yet. The argument only."

Step 4: Identify the one thing. "If the audience remembers only one sentence from this presentation, what should it be? Draft five candidates. Then select the strongest and explain why."

Step 5: Generate the slide-level outline. "Translate the argument spine into a slide-by-slide outline. For each slide: title as a claim rather than a topic label, one-sentence purpose, the content or data point that supports it, and any visual direction."

Step 6: Stress-test the narrative. "Read this outline as a skeptical audience member who has not pre-committed to the recommendation. Where does the argument lose them? Where is evidence missing? Where does the transition between sections feel unearned?"

Chapter 11: The Analysis & Research Playbook

Workflow 1: Research Synthesis

Step 1: Sharpen the question. "I need to conduct research to support the following decision: [decision description]. Before I bring you source materials, help me sharpen the research question. Ask me three clarifying questions that would make the question more specific and answerable. Then restate the question in its sharpest form."

Step 2: Frame the input set. "Here are the sources I have available: [list sources with type and recency]. Given the research question [restate], which of these are most likely to contain decision-relevant information? What gaps in the source set should I be aware of when interpreting the output?"

Step 3: Extract by question rather than by source. "I will now provide the following source materials. As you process each one, extract only information directly relevant to this question: [research question]. Organize extracted information by theme rather than by source. For each theme, note which sources agree, which conflict, and which add unique information the others do not."

Step 4: Surface the pattern. "Based on the extracted information, what is the strongest pattern in the data? What is the sharpest contradiction? What does the evidence most strongly support as an answer to the research question? State each in one sentence."

Step 5: Build the insight document. "Using the pattern and answer from Step 4, draft a structured insight document with these sections: Research Question, Key Findings (three to five bullets, each a specific claim supported by evidence), Significant Contradictions or Gaps, and Implication for Decision. Do not summarize sources. State conclusions supported by sources."

Step 6: Run the 'so what' audit. "Read the insight document as the person who needs to make the decision. After reading each section, state what question remains unanswered. Identify any finding where the implication for the decision is not explicit."

Workflow 2: Data Narrative

Step 1: Establish the decision context. "I have data I need to turn into a recommendation. The decision it needs to support is [specific decision]. The audience who will receive this is [role and background]. What they need to do after reading is [specific action or judgment]. Before I share the data, tell me: what would a strong recommendation for this audience look like? What structure, what level of detail, what balance of data versus interpretation?"

Step 2: Data intake with initial pattern prompt. "Here is the data: [paste]. Given the decision context, what is the dominant pattern? What is the clearest anomaly? What does the data most clearly support as a recommendation? State each in one sentence."

Step 3: Build the narrative spine. "Using the pattern and recommendation from Step 2, draft the narrative spine: (1) The situation the data describes, (2) The central finding in one sentence, (3) What the finding means for the decision, (4) The recommendation, (5) Supporting evidence in order of strength. Do not include all the data. Include the data that supports the recommendation."

Step 4: Test the recommendation against the data. "What alternative recommendations could reasonably be drawn from this same data? For each alternative, explain what assumption you would need to make and what evidence you would need to discount. Then explain why the primary recommendation is stronger."

Step 5: Translate for the audience. "Rewrite the recommendation section for an audience of [role]. Avoid [specific jargon]. The recommendation itself should not change, only how it is expressed."

Workflow 3: Competitive Intelligence

Step 1: Define the intelligence brief (one-time setup). "I need to build a competitive intelligence system for these competitors: [list]. The strategic question this intelligence should help me answer over time is [question]. The categories I want to track are [list]. For each category, what specific signals should I look for? I want observable changes that would be strategically meaningful rather than topics."

Step 2: Run a structured scan. "Using this intelligence brief [paste], analyze the following inputs for this period: [paste new information]. For each competitor, note: any signal from the brief categories, whether this signal represents a change from previous periods, and whether it confirms or contradicts a prior pattern."

Step 3: Pattern detection across periods. "Here are the competitive intelligence summaries from the past [N] periods: [paste]. What patterns have emerged across competitors that weren't visible in any single period? Which competitor is making the most significant strategic shift? What behavior from this period contradicts what you would have predicted based on prior periods?"

Step 4: Implication mapping. "Based on the patterns identified, what are the three most significant implications for our position? For each implication, state: what is at risk if we do not respond, what options are available, and what additional information would sharpen the assessment."

Step 5: Update the brief. "Based on this period's scan and pattern analysis, are there changes needed to the standing brief? Any new signals to add, categories producing noise without signal, or competitors to add or remove?"

Workflow 4: Decision Framework

Step 1: Decision decomposition. "I need to make the following decision: [description]. Before generating options, help me decompose it. What sub-decisions does this actually contain? What am I really deciding versus what are downstream consequences? What am I assuming is fixed that might actually be a variable?"

Step 2: Criteria weighting. "The criteria for this decision are [list]. For the primary decision-maker [role], rank these in order of the weight that would actually determine their choice if criteria conflicted, whatever their stated importance. Identify any criteria that appear on this list but would not actually change the decision regardless of how they score."

Step 3: Options generation. "Given the decision and criteria, generate four distinct options. For each: a name, a one-sentence description, performance against each criterion, and the assumption it depends on. Include at least one unconventional option well off the obvious path."

Step 4: Tradeoff mapping. "For each option, what does it sacrifice? What does it require to be true? What is the cost of being wrong? Present this as a tradeoff table: Option, What it gains, What it sacrifices, What must be true, Cost of failure."

Step 5: Stress-test the leading option. "Assume the leading option fails. What is the most likely reason? What early signal would indicate failure before it becomes irreversible? What is the fastest, lowest-cost way to test the core assumption before committing?"

Step 6: Draft the decision brief. "Draft a one-page decision brief: the decision, the criteria, four options with tradeoffs, and a recommendation with reasoning. State clearly which option is recommended and what must be true for it to succeed."

Chapter 12: The Strategy Playbook

Workflow 1: Scenario Planning

Step 1: Define the scenario space. "I am conducting scenario planning for this strategic question: [question]. The planning horizon is [timeframe]. The two variables I am most uncertain about, and that most affect the answer, are [Variable 1] and [Variable 2]. Before generating scenarios, confirm: are these the right two variables to use as scenario axes, or are there variables with higher uncertainty and higher impact I should be using? Explain your reasoning."

Step 2: Generate four scenarios from the axis matrix. "Using [Variable 1] and [Variable 2] as axes, generate four distinct scenarios by combining high and low outcomes for each variable. For each scenario: a name, what the world looks like in [timeframe], three highest-stakes implications for our strategic question, and a rough probability estimate. The four scenarios should be truly distinct rather than variations on the same theme."

Step 3: Stress-test each scenario. "For each scenario, what must be true for it to unfold as described? What early signals in the next six to twelve months would indicate this scenario is developing? What would cause you to revise its probability upward or downward?"

Step 4: Identify the robust strategy. "Given all four scenarios and their implications, what strategic direction performs adequately across the most scenarios? What bets are valid in only one or two scenarios and therefore carry high scenario risk? What decisions can be deferred until the scenario becomes clearer, and when must they be made?"

Step 5: Build the scenario summary. "Draft a scenario planning summary: the two driving uncertainties and why they were chosen, four scenarios with names and probability ranges, the robust strategic direction and why it holds across scenarios, and the two or three bets that are scenario-dependent with signals to watch."

Workflow 2: Assumption Audit

Step 1: Extract the recommendation's logic. "Here is a strategic recommendation I have developed: [recommendation and supporting rationale]. Do not evaluate it yet. First, extract the logical structure: what is the core claim, what evidence supports it, and what causal logic connects the evidence to the claim?"

Step 2: Surface the assumptions. "Now identify the assumptions embedded in this recommendation. Claims it depends on being true that the evidence does not establish. Look for assumptions about: market behavior, competitor response, organizational capability, resource availability, regulatory conditions, and timing. List every assumption you can identify."

Step 3: Rank by impact if wrong. "For each assumption, rate: (a) how likely is it to be wrong. High, medium, or low? and (b) if it is wrong, how severe is the impact on the recommendation. Critical (recommendation fails), significant (major revision required), or minor (holds with adjustment)? Organize by combined impact: critical first, regardless of likelihood."

Step 4: Challenge the critical assumptions. "For each assumption rated 'critical impact': what evidence would confirm it holds? What evidence would indicate it is wrong? Is that evidence available now or would we need to gather it? If we cannot confirm this assumption before implementation, what is the contingency?"

Step 5: Revise the recommendation to name its conditions. "Rewrite the recommendation to incorporate the critical assumptions as explicit conditions: 'This recommendation is valid if [Assumption A] holds. If [Assumption A] proves false, the recommended adjustment is [contingency]. The signal to watch is [early indicator].'"

Workflow 3: Strategic Brief

Step 1: Establish the audience standard. "The audience for this strategic brief is [role and profile]. What constitutes a credible strategic recommendation at their seniority and in their domain? What questions will they ask? What would make them trust the reasoning versus distrust it? What level of evidence do they require before acting?"

Step 2: Draft the recommendation statement. "Draft the core recommendation as a single declarative sentence. It should state: what we recommend doing, the primary reason, and the condition under which this recommendation holds. Avoid hedged language."

Step 3: Build the evidence architecture. "The recommendation is: [single sentence]. What evidence most directly supports it? Organize in three tiers: (1) evidence that directly confirms the recommendation, (2) evidence that makes alternatives less viable, (3) evidence that contextualizes without directly proving. Flag any gap in Tier 1. Claims where the recommendation rests on inference rather than direct evidence."

Step 4: Draft the brief. "Draft a strategic brief with these sections: Executive Summary (recommendation in one paragraph), Strategic Context, Recommendation (full statement with rationale), Evidence (three tiers as above), Critical Assumptions (top three from the assumption audit, ranked by impact), Contingency (what we do if critical assumptions fail), and Recommended Next Steps. Maximum [X] pages."

Step 5: Pressure-test the brief. "Read this brief as a skeptical senior leader disappointed by recommendations that sounded good but did not hold up. What do they question? Where does the reasoning feel thin? What would make them say 'I'm not convinced'?"

Workflow 4: Pre-Mortem

Step 1: Set the failure frame. "I am about to commit to the following plan: [description]. Assume it is [12 / 18 / 24] months from now and the plan has failed outright rather than partially succeeded. We did not achieve the objective. The resources were spent. Before explaining why it failed, tell me: what does failure look like concretely? What metrics or conditions would confirm failure rather than just difficulty?"

Step 2: Generate failure causes without constraints. "Now generate at least ten distinct reasons this plan could have failed, assuming failure has occurred. Include: execution failures, assumption failures, market failures, competitive responses, internal organizational failures, resource failures, and external events. Do not filter for likelihood. Include any cause that could plausibly produce the failure named in Step 1."

Step 3: Rank by preventability and impact. "For each failure cause, rate: (a) how likely is it. High, medium, or low? and (b) how preventable would it have been with prior action. Preventable, partially preventable, or unpreventable? Organize by highest impact and highest preventability combined. These are the failure modes worth acting on."

Step 4: Design preventive actions. "For each failure mode rated 'high impact and preventable': what specific action, taken before the plan launches, would reduce its probability? What monitoring system would give early warning? Who is responsible for each action and each monitoring signal?"

Step 5: Update the plan and assumption register. "Based on the pre-mortem findings, does the plan need to change before it is finalized? If yes, what specific changes address the highest-impact preventable failure modes? If the plan remains unchanged, what justifies accepting the identified failure risk?"

Chapter 13: The Leadership Playbook

Workflow 1: Performance Conversation Preparation

Step 1: Situation summary with relationship context. "I am preparing for a performance conversation with a direct report. Context: [role, tenure, performance level, specific situations]. My assessment: [honest assessment]. The outcome I need: [specific outcome]. Before developing any content, name: what is the core message this person needs to receive? What is the relationship dynamic I need to preserve while delivering it? Are these in tension, and if so, how?"

Step 2: Structure the conversation arc. "Given the core message and the relationship dynamic, recommend a conversation structure: how to open, how to move from opening to performance assessment, how to invite the employee's perspective, how to close with clarity about next steps and expectations. For each stage, give me the purpose and one or two sentences of guidance rather than a script."

Step 3: Draft key messages. "Draft the three or four core messages I need to convey. Each should: be specific rather than general; be behavioral, describing what the person did or did not do rather than who they are; carry the consequence or implication; and sound like how I actually speak. Here are my voice patterns: [Voice Anchors]."

Step 4: Prepare for the employee's likely responses. "What are the three most likely ways this employee responds to this conversation? For each: what does that response signal about how they are receiving the message? What is the appropriate next move: follow, redirect, or hold? What language allows me to do that without losing the conversation's direction?"

Step 5: Close with clarity. "Draft the closing statement, where I summarize what we discussed and what is expected going forward. It should be specific about expectations, clear about timeline, clear about what support is available, and clear about consequences if expectations are not met. Do not soften it to the point of ambiguity. After this conversation, this person should know exactly where they stand."

Workflow 2: Team Communication

Step 1: Establish the single source of truth. "I need to communicate [core message] to multiple audiences. Before adapting it, establish the single source of truth: what is the one thing every audience must understand after receiving this communication, regardless of framing? What would be lost if any audience received a materially different version?"

Step 2: Map the audience set. "I need to communicate to these groups: [list]. For each: what is their primary concern about this message? What context do they have that others don't? What do they need to hear first? What would make them feel excluded or misinformed if it was missing?"

Step 3: Generate audience-adapted versions. "Using the single source of truth and the audience profiles, draft a version for [Audience A]. The adaptation should: address their primary concern first, include context relevant to their role, preserve the core message exactly, and sound like me. Voice patterns: [Voice Anchors]. Do not adjust the substance. Adjust the framing."

Step 4: Consistency audit. "Here are the [N] audience-adapted versions: [paste all]. Confirm that every version: (a) conveys the same core message in substance; (b) makes no promise or implication in one version absent or contradicted in another; and (c) uses language that could not be quoted out of context in a way that contradicts another version."

Workflow 3: Coaching Prep

Step 1: Challenge the hypothesis. "I am preparing to coach [context]. Here is the situation they are working through: [description]. Here is my hypothesis about what they need: [hypothesis]. Challenge my hypothesis: what am I assuming about this person's situation that might not be accurate? What am I not seeing because I am looking through my own experience?"

Step 2: Generate a question bank. "Generate twelve to fifteen coaching questions for this situation. Organize them into four categories: questions that help the person clarify what they actually want (goal clarity); questions that help them see their situation more fully (reality check); questions that surface options they may not have considered (option generation); and questions about commitment and next steps (action). For each question, note what response it is designed to invite."

Step 3: Select the opening question. "From the question bank, which is the best opening for this specific person in this situation? Give me three candidates and explain why each would or would not work well as an opener."

Step 4: Prepare for the advice trap. "At what moment in this conversation am I most likely to shift from coaching to advising, moving from asking questions to giving answers? What is the trigger? What question could I ask instead of the advice I am likely to give?"

Step 5: Set the observation goal. "What is the one thing I most want to learn about this person from this conversation? Set aside helping them with this situation; I want to understand something about how they think or operate that will inform how I develop them over time."

Workflow 4: Stakeholder Alignment

Step 1: Stakeholder map. "I need to align stakeholders before announcing [decision]. The stakeholders are: [list with roles]. For each, assess: what is their current expectation about this decision? How does this announcement affect their interests? What is their likely initial reaction? What do they need to receive from me personally before the public announcement to feel respected rather than surprised?"

Step 2: Identify the alignment sequence. "Based on the stakeholder map, what is the optimal sequence for alignment conversations? Who must be aligned first because their support shapes how others respond? Who must not hear before [specific stakeholder] because it would damage a relationship? Who can be aligned in a group rather than individually, and what determines that?"

Step 3: Draft the anchor narrative. "The core message across all stakeholders is: [restate]. Draft the anchor narrative: what is happening, why, what it means for stakeholders generally, and what the process forward is. This narrative is consistent across alignment conversations and the eventual announcement. Individual conversations adapt the framing rather than the substance."

Step 5: Pre-announcement readiness check. "Before the announcement, confirm: have I had individual conversations with every stakeholder who would feel disrespected hearing this in a group? Is the anchor narrative consistent with everything I said in the alignment conversations? Are there any promises or framings made in private that contradict the public announcement? What questions am I most likely to receive at the announcement that I have not yet prepared for?"

Chapter 14: Director's Due Diligence

The Reputational Audit (saved-prompt template). Turn the six pre-send questions into one prompt the model runs on your draft before you do.

"Run a pre-send verification pass on the output below. (1) List every Tier 1 claim, meaning each specific figure, date, name, quotation, and attribution, and mark which can be confirmed against a primary source and which I still need to check. (2) For each Tier 2 conclusion, the inferences built on those facts, trace whether the premises actually support it. (3) For each Tier 3 recommendation, the synthesis, state what it assumes about the situation that might not hold. (4) Flag whether this output falls into any high-stakes category: factual claims with real consequences, legal or compliance material, personnel decisions, financial recommendations, or public-facing organizational content. (5) Name the single error that would most damage a professional relationship, a client's interests, or the organization's reputation if it slipped through undetected. (6) Tell me what I would need to verify before I could put my name on this and answer for it. Here is the output: [paste output]."

Check the one that would hurt most first. "In the output below, which single claim, if it were wrong, would embarrass me most in front of [audience]? Name it, and tell me how to verify it before anything else. [paste output]"

Make the model audit itself. "List every specific figure, date, and attribution in the output below, and rate your confidence in each from high to low. [paste output]"

The hostile fact-checker pass. "You are a hostile fact-checker with limited time. Read the output below. What do you attack first, and what would you need to see to believe it? [paste output]"

Rebuild the number from scratch (run on a second model). "Here are the raw inputs only: [paste the underlying data]. Reconstruct [the key figure] from these alone. Show your steps and your result, and do not refer to any prior analysis."

The Stale Check (Chapter 11, as a due-diligence reflex). "For each claim in the output below about a tool, price, model, or version, answer two things: how likely is this to be outdated, and how would I check it? [paste output]"
